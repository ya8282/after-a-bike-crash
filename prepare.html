Before the crash

The things that change how a bike crash goes are decided months earlier, in a quiet moment, for about an hour of effort and fifty dollars. This is that list. Most of it is free.

The first aid kit

The kit that helps is the one that is on the bike. A comprehensive kit in your hall cupboard has never treated anyone. Aim for something that lives permanently in a saddlebag or pannier and that you forget you own.

Two principles shape the list. First, a cyclist already carries water, a phone, and a multitool, so the kit only needs what those cannot do. Second, the injuries are predictable: road rash, a broken collarbone, a wrist, a head. Stock for those, not for a wilderness expedition.

Everyday kit

Roughly the size of a burrito. Fits a saddlebag or a jersey pocket. Under $30.

ItemWhy this one
Nitrile gloves, 2 pairsFor helping someone else, and for keeping grit out of your own open wound. Weighs nothing.

Skip the adhesive bandage assortment. A box of small plastic strips does nothing for a cycling injury, and it is most of what a drugstore kit contains, which is why drugstore kits are a poor starting point.

Check the kit twice a year. Ointment packets and medications expire, film dressings dry out, and things get borrowed and not replaced. Put it on the same schedule as changing your smoke alarm batteries.

Phone and device setup

Fifteen minutes, free, and the highest-value item on this page.

  • Medical ID, visible from the lock screen. iPhone: Health app, Medical ID, turn on "Show When Locked". Android: Settings, Safety & emergency, Medical info. Put in blood type, allergies, medications, conditions, and emergency contacts. Paramedics look for this.
  • Emergency SOS and fall or crash detection. iPhone and Apple Watch, Pixel, and Garmin and Wahoo head units can all call for help and send your location automatically if you go down and do not respond. Turn it on and test what it does.
  • Share your live location with someone for the ride, or use the incident detection in Strava, Garmin, or Ride with GPS. Tell them your route and rough return time.
  • Put an ICE contact in your contacts list, named so it sorts to the top, in addition to the Medical ID.
  • Photograph both sides of your ID, insurance cards, and your bike's serial number, and store them somewhere reachable from any device.
  • Save the numbers you will not be able to look up: your insurer's claims line, your doctor, your bike shop, and non-emergency police for the towns you ride through.
  • Keep the lock screen accessible. If a stranger cannot get your phone to show anything, they cannot call anyone for you.

Insurance, before you need it

This is the part people skip and regret. One phone call to your agent covers all of it.

  • Uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage, on your own auto policy, at the highest limit you can afford. This is the single most important line item for a cyclist. It typically covers you while riding a bike, and it is the main or only source of recovery when the driver flees or carries a minimum policy. Raising it is usually startlingly cheap. If you do not own a car, check whether a household member's policy covers you.
  • Medical payments coverage on the auto policy, which pays regardless of fault.
  • Know your health plan's deductible, out-of-pocket maximum, and urgent care and ER rules before you are choosing between them at the roadside.
  • Homeowner's or renter's insurance generally covers a damaged or stolen bike, subject to the deductible. For an expensive bike, ask about a scheduled item rider or a dedicated bicycle policy, and confirm whether crash damage is covered or only theft.
  • State-specific: in New York, a cyclist struck by a car claims no-fault benefits from that car's insurer, with a 30-day filing deadline. In New Jersey, PIP rules decide which policy pays, so ask now which one would be yours. Connecticut is fault-based, which is exactly why your own uninsured motorist limit matters most there.
  • An umbrella policy is worth asking about, both for what it adds to your uninsured motorist coverage and for the liability side, since a cyclist can also be the one at fault.

Documentation, done once

  • Photograph the bike from both sides, in good light, while it is undamaged, and photograph the serial number under the bottom bracket. This establishes condition and value.
  • Keep the receipts for the bike, the wheels, the helmet, the computer, and anything else that would be destroyed. A folder or an email label is enough.
  • Register the bike with Bike Index or 529 Garage, free, which also helps if it is stolen.
  • Write down what the bike and gear cost to replace today, not what you paid. Insurers ask, and people underestimate by half.

Cameras

A rear-facing camera is the most persuasive evidence a cyclist can produce, because the close pass and the rear-end collision are the crashes with no other witnesses. Front and rear units from Cycliq, Garmin, and others record on a loop.

The part people get wrong: loop recording overwrites the crash. If something happens, stop the camera, then pull the card out at home before you ride again. Copy the original file, not an edited clip, and keep it untouched. Do the same with phone video. Cameras on nearby buildings and buses are often overwritten within 72 hours, so knowing to ask immediately is worth as much as owning one yourself.

Skills worth an afternoon

  • Stop the Bleed is a free, roughly 90-minute class in bleeding control, widely offered by hospitals and fire departments. If you carry a tourniquet, this is not optional.
  • Red Cross or American Heart Association first aid and CPR, one day, teaches you what to do in the ten minutes before an ambulance arrives.
  • Know how to describe where you are. Practice reading a location off your phone's map, and note that a mile marker, a trail junction, or a cross street gets help to you faster than a street address you are guessing at.

Two prevention notes

This page is about the aftermath, not about how to ride, with two exceptions that change what the aftermath looks like.

A helmet that fits and sits level, replaced after any impact and roughly every five years, and a rear light running in daylight, which research consistently finds reduces being struck from behind. Neither prevents a driver from turning across you. Both change the injury you walk away with.

At the scene, collect

  • Driver's license, insurance card, and license plate, photographed
  • Every witness's name and phone number
  • Photos: whole scene, road, signals, your bike, your injuries
  • Nearby cameras: which buildings, ask them to save it
  • Officer's name, badge, and the report number

Do not say you are fine. Do not admit fault. Do not accept cash or settle at the curb. Do not let the driver leave before police arrive.
